# Whose Wages? A research draft

*Draft, November 1, 2026. Not published. This is the design for the arbitration between the Tightness Paradox result and Autor, Dube and McGrew, plus a first pass on public data. Nothing here has been run at full strength yet, and the draft says so where it matters.*

## 1. The question

The Tightness Paradox paper ends with one located effect. From 2022, in the third of states where hiring was hardest before COVID, hiring friction predicts higher state average wages: a coefficient of +0.650 in the frozen high-friction tercile, against null or negative coefficients in the other two thirds, with the groups frozen on 2015 to 2019 data and one pre-registered equality test rejecting at p = 0.0099. For the decade before, the same test finds nothing anywhere.

Autor, Dube and McGrew, in "The Unexpected Compression" (NBER Working Paper 31010, 2023, revised 2024), found the nearest published result. After the pandemic, labor market tightness became predictive of real wage growth for low-wage workers across states. Their mechanism is a job ladder: where markets ran hottest, workers climbed to better-paying employers, pay at the bottom rose fastest, and the distribution compressed from below.

So the arbitration question is one sentence:

**Is the high-friction states' average-wage effect just the compression channel showing up in the mean, or does the wage response reach workers and industries the ladder story does not cover?**

Three answers are possible, and each one does something different to the main paper.

**Subsumed.** If the effect sits entirely in low-wage industries, in low percentiles of the wage distribution, and in the earnings of workers who switched jobs, then there is one post-COVID fact, not two. Tightness and friction are correlated measures, the ladder is the mechanism, and the friction coefficient is that mechanism read through a noisier variable. The honest move is to say so in the main paper, cite the arbitration, and demote the located effect from a finding to a confirmation. The landscape memo's falsification line already commits to this in writing: the result gets reported as a comment-style note, not a standalone claim.

**Distinct.** If the effect is spread across industry wage tiers, visible at the middle and top of the distribution, and still there when job-to-job switching is held constant, then friction has its own channel. The reading would be that an opening which stays unfilled in a state where openings pile up is itself a bid: the employer who cannot hire at the posted wage raises the wage on the job, for the worker already in it and the next one alike, without anyone having to switch employers first. That partitions the post-COVID wage story between the two papers: the ladder explains the bottom, conversion failure explains a broader pressure in the places where hiring was already failing.

**Complementary.** The middle outcome is that the effect is partly ladder and partly not: concentrated at the bottom and the lower middle, say, or strong in switching-heavy industries and present but weaker elsewhere. Then the two results are one story told at two margins, and the follow-up's job is to draw the boundary precisely: which workers, which industries, how much of the +0.650 each channel carries. This is the most likely outcome on priors, and it is still worth having, because right now nobody has drawn that boundary for the friction measure at all.

The draft below designs all three tests, states in advance what pattern produces which verdict, and reports a first pass on the cheapest one.

## 2. The data

Everything here is published data from the publisher. No series needs a key except where flagged.

### 2.1 Wages by state and industry: QCEW

- **Publisher and product:** U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, Quarterly Census of Employment and Wages (QCEW).
- **Series:** average annual pay and annual average employment by state and NAICS sector (aggregation level 54, total all ownerships), and quarterly average weekly wage by state and industry for the panel version.
- **Frequency:** quarterly releases plus annual averages. The annual single-file CSVs are public at data.bls.gov/cew/data/files/{year}/csv/{year}_annual_singlefile.zip; quarterly files sit in the same directory tree. No key, no account.
- **Role:** the industry-tier test (section 3a). Industry average pay is the tier variable: an industry is "low-wage" because its national average pay is low, which is a statement about the industry's job mix, not about any individual worker. That gap between low-wage industries and low-wage workers is the single largest weakness of this test and section 4 returns to it.
- **Known limits:** QCEW pay is total wages divided by average employment, so it moves with hours, bonuses, and within-industry composition, not just wage rates. Government (NAICS 92) is published under separate ownership codes and is excluded from the first pass; the full build should either add it back under its own ownership rows or state the exclusion as a scope choice. Disclosure-suppressed cells (flag "N") drop out, which is why cell counts below are under the full 51 by 19 grid.

### 2.2 The switching margin: LEHD Job-to-Job Flows and QWI

- **Publisher and product:** U.S. Census Bureau, Longitudinal Employer-Household Dynamics (LEHD) program. Two products.
- **J2J (Job-to-Job Flows):** statistics on job mobility built from linked employer-employee administrative records: job-to-job transition rates, hires and separations to and from employment, and, critically for this draft, **earnings changes due to job change** and the characteristics of origin and destination jobs. Published at national, state, and metropolitan level, by worker and firm characteristics. **Quarterly**, from 2000 forward (state coverage builds over the early years). Access: J2J Explorer and the LED Extraction Tool at lehd.ces.census.gov, plus bulk CSV downloads from the same site. No key.
- **QWI (Quarterly Workforce Indicators):** 32 indicators on employment, hires, separations, job creation and destruction, and earnings, by geography, industry, age, sex, race, ethnicity, and education. The earnings measures that matter here are average monthly earnings for stable jobs and for new hires, which give a published, quarterly read on whether wage action sits with movers or with people who stayed. **Quarterly**, back to 1990 for early partner states. Same access routes as J2J.
- **Role:** test (b). J2J's job-to-job flow rate by state and quarter is the conditioning variable: the administrative, near-universe version of the tracker's switcher share, at exactly the geography the design needs. J2J's published earnings gains for job changers are the direct check on the ladder's payoff margin.
- **Known limits:** LEHD is administrative, so it inherits state UI coverage rules and reporting lags; the public files trail real time by several quarters. Some state-quarter cells are missing in the historical J2J files and have to be treated as gaps, not zeros. Earnings in LEHD are quarterly UI earnings, not hourly wage rates, so they mix hours and rates the same way QCEW does.

### 2.3 The distribution: CPS MORG, ACS PUMS, OEWS

The percentile test needs a state wage distribution over time. Three public routes exist, in descending order of fit.

- **CPS Merged Outgoing Rotation Groups (MORG).** Publisher: U.S. Census Bureau and BLS (Current Population Survey); harmonized extracts via **IPUMS CPS** (free account required) or the **NBER MORG extracts** (public, no account). Monthly microdata; the earnings questions go to the outgoing rotation groups, roughly a quarter of the CPS sample each month. Roughly 280,000 individuals a year appear in the MORG files, about half with reported earnings, so about 140,000 earnings observations a year nationally. **The state arithmetic is the constraint:** that is an average of roughly 2,700 earnings observations per state per year, running from a few hundred in the smallest states to well over ten thousand in California. A state-year median is fine on that. A state-year 90th percentile in Wyoming is a couple of dozen people above the line and should not be published as a point estimate. Weekly earnings are top-coded ($2,885 a week in recent files), which binds at the top in high-wage states. The workable design is ADM's own: pool to half-years or years, compute percentiles within frozen state groups rather than state by state, and use state-level percentiles only for the large states as a check. ADM's paper is itself built on CPS state samples pooled this way, so the route is proven for exactly this kind of question.
- **ACS PUMS.** Publisher: U.S. Census Bureau, American Community Survey 1-year Public Use Microdata Sample. Annual person-level wage and salary income (INCWAGE) for a roughly 1 percent sample of the population. This is the best-powered percentile route for levels: state samples are large enough that p10 through p90 by state and year are stable except in the very smallest states, and it covers the full decade. Limits: annual only, income refers to the prior 12 months on a rolling basis (so the "2022" ACS wage is a moving window, not a calendar year), no hourly rate (weeks and hours are reported, so an hourly construct is possible but noisy), and no way to identify job switchers. Role: the levels cross-check on the MORG percentiles, and the primary source if the MORG samples prove too thin in the states that matter.
- **OEWS.** Publisher: BLS, Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics. Annual (May reference) percentile wages, the 10th, 25th, 50th, 75th, and 90th, published by **occupation within state**, from an establishment survey. A state distribution can be approximated by aggregating occupation percentiles with occupation employment weights, but percentiles do not aggregate exactly, and BLS warns that OEWS estimates are built from rotating panels pooled over three years and are not designed as a time series. Role: robustness only, and a source for occupation-mix controls. Not the primary percentile source.

### 2.4 What quarterly does to the design

The main paper's test is monthly: a state monthly panel, January 2022 to December 2025, friction lagged three months, log real wages, one equality test across frozen terciles. Every arbitration data source except the CPS is quarterly, and the monthly state JOLTS series itself ends with December 2025. So the arbitration cannot be bolted onto the monthly regression. It needs its own statement:

- **Quarterly restatement.** Aggregate the monthly friction proxy to quarters (the within-quarter mean, same lag logic shifted to one quarter), take quarterly wages from QCEW (state average weekly wage) or QWI (stable-job earnings), keep the same frozen 2015 to 2019 terciles, and run the same single equality test on 2022Q1 to 2025Q4: 16 quarters, 51 states. The estimand is the quarterly cousin of +0.650, not the number itself, and the draft will not pretend otherwise. If the located effect does not survive its own quarterly restatement, that is a result, and it is reported before any arbitration test runs.
- **What is lost:** the fine timing (the three-month lag becomes one quarter, the post-2022 break becomes a quarter boundary), and some power (48 monthly observations per state become 16 quarterly ones).
- **What is gained:** every arbitration variable (industry wages, J2J flows, QWI earnings) is natively quarterly, so conditioning is exact rather than interpolated.

## 3. The tests

Each test states its estimand and its falsification line in advance, in the main paper's discipline. The three tests run in order; test (a) is the cheapest and is already partly done (section 4).

### 3a. The tier gradient (industry composition)

**Design.** A state by industry by quarter panel, 2015Q1 to 2025Q4. Outcome: log real average wage in the state-industry cell (QCEW). Regressor: the industry's **national** unfilled share (openings minus hires over openings, from the main paper's industry panel, lagged one quarter), interacted with a post-2022 indicator and with the state's frozen friction tercile. State by industry and quarter fixed effects. Industries are split into wage tiers by their 2021 national average pay (QCEW), low, mid, and high, frozen, the same trick as the state terciles. One design fact to confirm at build time: published state JOLTS does not give full state-by-industry conversion, so the friction assigned to a state-industry cell is the industry's national conversion failure, exactly as in the main paper's industry test. The identifying variation is therefore: within a high-friction state after 2022, do the industries that nationally fail to convert pay more, and does that gradient differ by the industry's wage tier?

**Estimand.** The post-2022 unfilled-share gradient in high-friction states, by tier: beta_low, beta_mid, beta_high, and the two contrasts beta_low minus beta_mid and beta_low minus beta_high.

**Falsification line.** If beta_low is positive and beta_mid and beta_high are zero within their intervals, the mean effect is a low-wage-industry composition fact, and on this test the compression account absorbs it. If beta_mid or beta_high is positive and materially different from zero, the effect reaches past the industries where the ladder does its work, and this test votes distinct. A flat gradient (all three equal) votes distinct on composition while saying nothing about workers, which is what tests (b) and (c) are for.

### 3b. The switching condition (the ladder itself)

**Design.** The quarterly state panel from section 2.4. Outcome: log real state wage. Regressors: the friction proxy interacted with the frozen terciles, the main paper's controls, state and quarter fixed effects. Then add the J2J job-to-job flow rate for the state and quarter (job-to-job transitions per job, published by LEHD), and its interaction with the post-2022 indicator. A secondary version replaces the flow rate with J2J's published average earnings gain for job changers in the state, which is the ladder's payoff measured directly. A second secondary check uses QWI: rerun the outcome as new-hire earnings and as stable-job earnings separately. The ladder story predicts the action concentrates in new-hire earnings; a friction story predicts stable-job earnings move too, because the raise lands on the job, not only on the mover.

**Estimand.** The high-tercile friction coefficient before and after conditioning on job-to-job flows: its size, its interval, and the attenuation share.

**Falsification line, set now:** if conditioning on the J2J flow rate cuts the high-tercile coefficient by three quarters or more and leaves it statistically indistinguishable from zero at the 10 percent level, the switching margin carries the effect and the verdict on this test is subsumed. If the coefficient keeps at least half its size and its significance with flows in the regression, this test votes distinct: whatever friction is doing, it is not only paying people to move. Anything between those lines is reported as the complementary split, with the attenuation share itself as the headline number.

### 3c. The percentile version (workers, not industries)

**Design.** From CPS MORG (IPUMS or NBER extracts), pool earnings observations to years (half-years as a check, following ADM's construction), and compute real weekly-earnings percentiles p10, p25, p50, p75, p90 **within each frozen tercile group**, 2015 to 2025, using earnings weights. The object is the group's percentile growth profile in the two eras: 2015 to 2019 and 2021 to 2025 (2021 as the base year, matching the first pass in section 4; the pandemic years 2020 and 2021 are shown, not hidden, but the headline contrast is pre versus post). ACS PUMS percentiles by group are the cross-check. The regression version, if samples allow, is ADM's own cell structure: percentile-group wage growth on group friction exposure, era by era.

**Estimand.** The high-friction group's percentile growth profile after 2022, relative to the low-friction group's, percentile by percentile: the differences (high group minus low group) at p10, p25, p50, p75, p90.

**Falsification line.** ADM's channel has a signature: the differences decline monotonically in the percentile, large at p10 and p25, gone by p75. If the estimated profile has that shape, the worker-level evidence votes subsumed, whatever the industry test said, because this is the test that measures workers. If the differences are roughly flat across percentiles, or rise into the middle, the effect is a broad wage pressure in high-friction states and votes distinct. If the profile is positive at the bottom and the middle and zero at the top, that is the complementary partition, drawn at the percentile where it dies.

## 4. First pass: the industry tiers, on QCEW annual data

The tier test has a cheap descriptive version that needs no regression: split the frozen groups' wage growth by industry wage tier and look at the shape. That version has been run, on published data, and it is reported here as preliminary in the strict sense: descriptive, nominal, annual, and composition-laden.

**What was run.** BLS QCEW annual single files for 2015, 2019, 2021, and 2025 (public CSVs, pulled November 1, 2026). Statewide NAICS-sector rows, total all ownerships, disclosure-suppressed cells dropped, NAICS 92 (government) and 99 (unclassified) excluded: 3,648 state-sector-year cells. Industries assigned to tiers by 2021 national employment-weighted average annual pay. Low tier: accommodation and food services ($25,685), retail trade, agriculture, other services, arts and recreation, administrative and waste services, transportation and warehousing (up to $57,969). Mid tier: health care and social assistance, educational services, construction, real estate, manufacturing, wholesale trade. High tier: mining, professional and technical services, utilities, finance and insurance, management of companies, information (up to $152,877). For each frozen state group and tier, average annual pay growth is the base-year-employment-weighted mean of log pay changes across the group's state-industry cells. Nominal dollars throughout: a national price deflator is common to every cell in a window, so it cancels in every cross-group and cross-tier contrast, which are the only contrasts read here. Script: whose_wages_run.py, outputs whose_wages_group_tier.csv, whose_wages_by_industry.csv, whose_wages_tiers.csv, alongside this draft. The headline cells of the table below were independently recomputed from the filtered file and match.

**The table.** Employment-weighted nominal average annual pay growth, percent, 2021 to 2025:

| Frozen group | Low tier | Mid tier | High tier | Low minus high (pp) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| High friction | +14.7 | +16.1 | +17.8 | −3.1 |
| Mid friction | +16.3 | +15.6 | +19.8 | −3.5 |
| Low friction | +17.2 | +18.0 | +18.8 | −1.6 |

Placebo window, 2015 to 2019, same construction: high-friction group +14.3 / +9.3 / +12.4 (low minus high +1.9); low-friction group +11.1 / +9.6 / +11.9 (low minus high −0.8). The middle window, 2019 to 2021: high-friction +14.6 / +10.4 / +14.2 (+0.3); low-friction +14.3 / +9.8 / +11.8 (+2.5).

**What it says, and what it does not.**

First, the crude subsumption story gets no support here. If the high-friction states' average-wage strength were simply low-wage industries surging, their 2021 to 2025 growth should tilt toward the low tier relative to other states. It does not. In every group, high-wage industries' average pay grew fastest over the window, and the high-friction group's low-minus-high spread (−3.1 points) sits between the mid group's (−3.5) and the low group's (−1.6). Relative to low-friction states, the high-friction states' growth was, if anything, slightly more tilted toward high-wage industries, by about 1.5 points of spread. Industry by industry, high-friction states grew more slowly than low-friction states in 17 of 19 sectors (finance and insurance, +2.2 points, and accommodation and food services, +0.6, are the exceptions). Overall pay growth was slower in the high-friction group (+15.9 percent against +17.8 percent, all industries), which is not in tension with the main paper: that paper estimates a slope on friction in monthly real wages, not a ranking of group growth levels, and QCEW annual pay per job is a different object from the paper's wage series besides.

Second, the limits, stated as plainly as the result. This pass measures **industries, not workers**. ADM's compression is a fact about low-wage workers, and low-wage workers work in every industry, including high-pay ones; a hospital's mean pay can rise because its executives did. Sector average pay moves with occupational mix, hours, and bonuses (finance and information pay over this window is substantially a bonus and equity story). Annual averages blur the paper's 2022 break. And this is a description of growth rates, not the friction regression: no unfilled-share regressor appears anywhere in it. What the pass actually rules out is one narrow claim, that the located effect is an artifact of low-wage **sectors** booming in high-friction states. It is not. The worker-level question stays exactly as open as it was, which is why tests (b) and (c) exist. Anyone citing this table as evidence against ADM is citing it for more than it says; the draft's own falsification lines are in section 3, and none of them has been run yet.

## 5. Verdict

**Build it as a standalone research note on the desk, structured so it can fold into the main paper as an arbitration appendix without rework. Do not build it as a section of the main paper, and do not leave it as an unpublished appendix.**

Reasons. The quarterly restatement (section 2.4) means the arbitration lives in a different design from the main paper's monthly test: different frequency, different wage sources, three data programs the main paper never touches. Folding that into the main paper's body would blur the cleanest thing the main paper has, which is one design, frozen, run once. As a standalone note it gets its own statement of estimands and falsification lines, published before the results, in the same discipline as the main paper's pre-registration, and the main paper links to it the way it already links to the Vintage Test: one sentence, "whether the effect is the ladder or something broader is tested separately, here." If the verdict comes back subsumed, the note is already in the comment form the landscape memo prescribed, and the main paper's sentence changes from "tested separately" to "tested, and absorbed." If it comes back distinct or complementary, the note stands as the second paper in a series the desk can point to, and the partition it draws becomes the premise for whatever comes next.

Estimated work to finish, in desk sessions rather than calendar time:

1. **Data pulls, one to two sessions.** QCEW is already pulled and scripted (whose_wages_run.py; the quarterly panel version is an extension of the same pull). QWI and J2J come from the LED Extraction Tool or bulk files; allow a session for the extraction tool's query limits and the missing-cell map. MORG is the slow one: an IPUMS extract needs Kevin's account (or the NBER extracts, which need none), and the percentile construction with top-code handling is half a session of care on its own. ACS PUMS cross-check files are a direct download.
2. **Estimation, two sessions.** The quarterly restatement first, reported on its own before any arbitration test, then tests (a) and (b) on the quarterly panel (the code is a close cousin of the main paper's grouped regressions), then test (c) on the pooled MORG percentiles.
3. **Build and voice pass, one session.** The note in the desk's design language, charts from the three tests, Kevin's editing pass before anything is final, per the standing rule.

Total: roughly four to six working sessions, with the MORG extract the only step that waits on anything outside the desk. The decision the note needs from Kevin before it starts is small: the falsification lines in sections 3b and 3c are stated as commitments, and they should be published with the design, before the estimates exist. That is what makes the answer worth believing in either direction.
